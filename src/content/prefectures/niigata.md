Niigata faces the Sea of Japan across from the mountains that catch
Siberian winter storms and wring them out as snow — enough snow that parts
of the prefecture regularly rank among the snowiest inhabited places on
Earth, a fact that shapes almost everything about how the region farms,
brews, and markets itself.

### Niigata and the wider world

Niigata's formal opening to the world came early: it was named one of
five original treaty ports in Japan's 1858 commercial treaties with the
US and European powers, alongside Yokohama, Kobe, Nagasaki, and Hakodate
— though its shallow harbor limited how much international shipping
actually developed here compared to the others.

Its more lasting global reputation came through literature. Yasunari
Kawabata's 1948 novel "Snow Country," largely set in the hot spring town
of Echigo-Yuzawa, was among the works cited when he became the first
Japanese author to win the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1968 — the
novel's spare, atmospheric prose about the region's snow and hot springs
is still one of the more widely translated pieces of modern Japanese
fiction internationally. Separately, Sado Island's former gold mine —
once among the world's largest gold-mining operations — was added to the
UNESCO World Heritage list in 2024, a listing that involved genuine
diplomatic discussion with South Korea over the site's wartime labor
history.

### Neighbors: Niigata and the rest of Chubu

Niigata anchors the northern, Sea of Japan-facing edge of the Chubu
region, geographically and culturally somewhat separate from Chubu's
Pacific-facing and inland prefectures (like Nagano and Aichi) — closer,
in climate and outlook, to Tohoku's Sea of Japan coast than to Chubu's
mountainous interior.

## Getting there and getting around

Niigata Station and Echigo-Yuzawa Station are both on the Joetsu
Shinkansen, roughly 1.5-2 hours from Tokyo. Sado Island is reached by
ferry or jetfoil from Niigata City's port.
