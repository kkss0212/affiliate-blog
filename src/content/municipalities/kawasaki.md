Kawasaki grew up along the Tokaido road as a post town, but its modern
identity was set in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, when its
position directly on Tokyo Bay between the capital and Yokohama's port made
it a natural site for heavy industry. The city became a core part of the
Keihin Industrial Zone, one of Japan's most important manufacturing belts,
home over the decades to steelmaking, shipbuilding, and petrochemical
plants alongside major manufacturers like Fujifilm and, historically, Nikon.

### From pollution problem to night-view attraction

That industrial concentration came with real costs — Kawasaki was one of the
more visible sites of Japan's postwar industrial pollution problems before
stricter environmental regulation in the 1970s improved conditions
considerably. In an unexpected reversal, the same illuminated refineries and
steel plants that once symbolized the city's pollution issues have become a
minor but genuine tourist draw in recent decades, with dedicated bus and
boat tours built around viewing the industrial skyline lit up at night — a
niche now shared by other Japanese industrial ports like Yokkaichi.

### Kawasaki between two giants

Kawasaki's defining geographic fact is its position: wedged directly between
Tokyo and Yokohama, with no clear center of gravity of its own the way
either neighbor has. Kawasaki Daishi, a major Shingon Buddhist temple whose
New Year's visitor numbers regularly rank among Japan's highest, is one of
the city's few destinations with genuinely independent draw rather than
serving mostly as a stop between its two larger neighbors.

## Getting there and getting around

Kawasaki Station sits on the JR Tokaido Line between Tokyo and Yokohama,
about 20 minutes from central Tokyo. The city is compact and well served by
JR and private railway lines, making it an easy add-on to a Tokyo or
Yokohama itinerary rather than typically a standalone destination.
