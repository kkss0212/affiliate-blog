Yamaguchi occupies the far western tip of Honshu, and for a domain that
size, played an outsized role in ending nearly 700 years of samurai
government.

### Yamaguchi and the wider world

In 1863, at genuine personal risk — leaving Japan without permission was
a capital offense under the shogunate's sakoku policy — five young
Choshu samurai secretly boarded a British ship and sailed to London to
study Western science, engineering, and naval technology firsthand.
Known afterward as the Choshu Five, several went on to become central
figures in the Meiji government they helped bring about: Ito Hirobumi
became Japan's first prime minister and principal author of its first
constitution, while others led the founding of Japan's modern navy,
mint, and industrial ministries. Their unauthorized voyage is often cited
as a turning point in convincing Choshu's leadership that adopting
Western knowledge, not resisting it, was the domain's best path forward
— a conviction that helped drive Choshu, alongside Satsuma (modern
Kagoshima), to lead the movement that overthrew the shogunate in 1868.

Yamaguchi's international role didn't end there: in 1895, the Treaty of
Shimonoseki — negotiated in the city of the same name — formally ended
the First Sino-Japanese War, marking Japan's emergence as a recognized
regional military power for the first time.

### Neighbors: Yamaguchi and Kyushu

Shimonoseki sits directly across the narrow Kanmon Straits from Kyushu,
close enough to Fukuoka that the two are linked by both an undersea
tunnel and a bridge — making Yamaguchi, geographically and historically,
as much a gateway to Kyushu as it is the western edge of Honshu.

## Getting there and getting around

Shin-Yamaguchi and Shin-Shimonoseki are both Sanyo Shinkansen stops,
roughly 4.5-5 hours from Tokyo. Iwakuni and Akiyoshido are best reached
by local train or bus from Shin-Yamaguchi or Hiroshima.
