Shiga sits just east of Kyoto, close enough that it's long served as a
transit corridor for travelers and trade heading in and out of the old
capital — the historic Tokaido and Nakasendo routes both ran through or
near the prefecture, and the "Omi shonin" merchant class that grew up
along them became famous for a business philosophy still cited in Japan
today: that a good deal should benefit the seller, the buyer, and society
at large.

### Shiga and the wider world

Shiga's most consequential single encounter with the outside world came
in 1891, in an incident that had nothing to do with trade. Nicholas
Alexandrovich, the Tsarevich of Russia (and future Tsar Nicholas II), was
touring Japan when a Japanese police officer assigned to guard him
attacked him with a sword in the city of Otsu, wounding him in the head.
The attack — feared at the time to risk provoking war with Russia — sent
the Meiji government into crisis; the emperor personally visited the
recovering Tsarevich, and enormous political pressure was applied to have
the attacker executed. Instead, the presiding judge, Kojima Iken, ruled
that ordinary criminal law (which did not prescribe death for attacks on
foreign dignitaries) had to apply regardless of the political stakes,
sentencing the man to life imprisonment. The "Otsu Incident," as it's
still known, is widely taught in Japan today as an early landmark for
judicial independence from political interference.

### Neighbors: Shiga and the rest of Kansai

Shiga borders Kyoto directly and has long functioned as its practical
gateway to the rest of Japan, with Lake Biwa itself serving historically
as a major inland shipping route connecting Kyoto's hinterland to routes
further east.

## Getting there and getting around

Otsu and Hikone are both reachable from Kyoto by regular train in under
an hour; Maibara, in Shiga, is a Tokaido Shinkansen stop. Lake Biwa's
shoreline is well served by local rail on both its east and west sides.
