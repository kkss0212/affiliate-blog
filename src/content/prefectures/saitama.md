Saitama borders Tokyo directly to the north and has grown, over the last
century, almost entirely in that shadow — from farmland into one of
Japan's most densely populated commuter prefectures, without ever quite
shaking a national reputation for having no distinct identity of its own.

### Saitama and the wider world

That reputation became the entire premise of a movie: "Fly Me to Saitama"
(2019), a broad, self-aware comedy imagining a Japan where Tokyoites
openly discriminate against people from Saitama, became a genuine box
office hit and won over the very prefecture it was mocking, spawning a
2023 sequel. It's a useful shortcut for understanding how Saitama sees
itself relative to its famous neighbor: in on the joke, not especially
offended by it.

More concretely, Saitama has hosted the world directly: when Japan and
South Korea co-hosted the 2002 FIFA World Cup, newly built Saitama
Stadium held several matches, including a semifinal — one of the larger
global sporting events Japan has staged, and a rare moment of
international spotlight landing squarely on the prefecture rather than
passing through it toward Tokyo.

### Neighbors: Saitama and Tokyo

Saitama's entire modern identity is defined by its relationship to Tokyo
— administratively separate, economically inseparable, and the subject
of enough gentle Tokyo-centric condescension that Saitama residents
turned it into a hit movie rather than a grievance.

## Getting there and getting around

Omiya Station, a major Shinkansen junction, is about 30 minutes from
Tokyo Station; Kawagoe is reachable directly from central Tokyo by
regular train in under an hour. Chichibu requires a longer train or
express bus connection.
