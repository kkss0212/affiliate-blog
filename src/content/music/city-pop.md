City Pop doesn't have a single founder or a clean start date so much as a
moment when a handful of Japanese artists started reaching for the same
sound at once: glossy, funk-inflected soft rock aimed at a newly affluent,
increasingly car-owning middle class in the bubble-economy years leading
into the 1980s. It's music built for a very specific mental image — driving
along a coastal road at night, city lights reflected in a windshield — and
that mood has turned out to travel far better than anyone involved could
have predicted at the time.

### An accidental second life on YouTube

City Pop's most consequential "wider world" moment happened decades after
the genre's commercial peak, and nobody planned it. Starting around 2017,
Mariya Takeuchi's 1984 track "Plastic Love" began circulating widely on
YouTube, picked up largely through the platform's recommendation algorithm
rather than any coordinated reissue campaign — a genuinely bottom-up
rediscovery that pulled a wave of listeners outside Japan toward the
broader genre. Tatsuro Yamashita, Anri, Tomoko Aran, and other artists from
the same era found new international audiences on the strength of that one
song's algorithmic momentum, and City Pop has stayed in steady rotation on
retro-leaning playlists and reissue labels ever since — an unusually direct
example of a 40-year-old regional genre finding a genuinely global second
audience through nothing but internet recommendation systems.

## Where to start

Mariya Takeuchi's "Plastic Love," from the 1984 album *VARIETY*, is the
obvious entry point — it's the song responsible for the genre's global
rediscovery, and still the clearest single example of what City Pop sounds
like at its most polished. From there, Tatsuro Yamashita's catalog (his
1980 album *Ride on Time* in particular) fills in the sound's other major
pillar, and a broader "City Pop" playlist on any major streaming service
will surface the genre's wider cast — Anri, Tomoko Aran, and others working
the same late-night, funk-driven register.
