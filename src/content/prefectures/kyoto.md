For a thousand years — from 794 until the emperor moved to Tokyo in 1868 — Kyoto
was simply "the capital" (京, kyō). Everything the rest of the world associates
with premodern Japanese court culture — its poetry, its tea ceremony, its
formal gardens, the strict layering of kimono — was, more or less, invented
or codified here, in a city laid out on a Chinese-style grid (modeled on the
Tang dynasty capital of Chang'an) that you can still walk today.

### Kyoto and the wider world

Kyoto's relationship with the West is a strange, late-arriving one. During
Japan's roughly 220 years of *sakoku* (closed-country policy, 1639–1853),
almost all foreign contact was funneled through the port of Nagasaki, not
Kyoto — the old capital stayed deliberately inward-facing. Its most famous
brush with the West came in 1945, under much darker circumstances: Kyoto sat
on the U.S. military's original shortlist of atomic bomb targets, and was
removed largely due to the personal objections of Secretary of War Henry
Stimson, who reportedly knew and admired the city from his honeymoon there
decades earlier. Whatever the exact mix of reasons, the effect is not in
dispute — Kyoto is the reason Japan still has an intact pre-industrial
capital at all.

The postwar relationship has been considerably friendlier: Kyoto is where
the 1997 Kyoto Protocol on climate change was negotiated and signed, and the
city maintains sister-city relationships with Boston, Paris, Florence,
Cologne, and Prague, among others — an unusually European-heavy list for a
Japanese prefecture.

### Neighbors: Kyoto, Osaka, and Tokyo

Within the Kansai region, Kyoto and Osaka have a friendly, well-worn rivalry
that shows up constantly in Japanese pop culture: Kyoto is cast as the
refined, tradition-bound elder, Osaka as the loud, funny, merchant-minded
younger sibling 50 km down the rail line. The more consequential relationship
is with Tokyo. When the imperial capital moved there in 1868 as part of the
Meiji Restoration, Kyoto lost its political center of gravity for the first
time in over a millennium — a demotion the city answered by leaning fully
into culture, craft, and (eventually) tourism instead.

## Getting there and getting around

Kyoto Station is a shinkansen stop on the Tokaido line, about 2 hours 15
minutes from Tokyo and 15 minutes from Osaka — making it an easy day trip
from either, though it rewards staying longer. The city itself is compact
and mostly flat, well served by buses and a small subway network, and
genuinely pleasant to cover by rental bicycle outside of peak summer heat.
