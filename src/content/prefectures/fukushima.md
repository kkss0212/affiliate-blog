Fukushima is large enough to contain three fairly distinct regions —
coastal Hamadori, central Nakadori (where the capital sits), and
mountainous, inland Aizu — and most of what defines the prefecture's
older identity comes from Aizu, whose samurai loyalty to the Tokugawa
shogunate made it one of the last and most determined holdouts against
the new Meiji government in 1868.

### Fukushima and the wider world

That resistance had a genuine European connection. During the Boshin War,
France had been arming and training the shogunate's forces before its
collapse, and at least one French military advisor, Jules Brunet,
resigned his commission rather than abandon his Japanese allies —
fighting alongside domains sympathetic to the old order, including forces
connected to Aizu, even after his own government officially withdrew
support. Brunet's story is often cited as one of the inspirations behind
the 2003 film "The Last Samurai," though that film's plot is fictional
and not a direct retelling.

The prefecture's more recent, much heavier-weight international moment
came on March 11, 2011, when the Tohoku earthquake and tsunami triggered
a meltdown at the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear plant on the coast — an event
that became globally synonymous with the prefecture's name. It's worth
being precise about scale: the affected area is a specific stretch of
coastline, not the whole prefecture, which is roughly the size of
Connecticut and Rhode Island combined. Extensive monitoring, decontamination,
and rebuilding since 2011 have reopened the large majority of the
prefecture, including Aizu and most of Nakadori, to normal life and
travel; a shrinking evacuation zone remains in place immediately around
the plant itself. Check current official guidance for the latest status
before planning a visit to the coastal Hamadori area specifically.

### Neighbors: Fukushima and the rest of Tohoku

Fukushima is Tohoku's southernmost and closest prefecture to Tokyo,
functioning as something of a gateway between the Kanto and Tohoku
regions — closer to the capital, in both distance and, at times,
cultural feel, than Tohoku's more northern prefectures.

## Getting there and getting around

Fukushima Station and Koriyama Station are both Tohoku Shinkansen stops,
roughly 1.5-2 hours from Tokyo. Aizuwakamatsu is reached by local train
from Koriyama; a rental car is useful for the Bandai-Goshikinuma area.
