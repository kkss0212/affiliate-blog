Kitakyushu doesn't have a single founding story the way most Japanese
cities do — it was assembled deliberately, in 1963, out of five separate
existing cities (Moji, Kokura, Wakamatsu, Yahata, and Tobata) as part of a
national push to consolidate Japan's industrial base into larger, more
administratively powerful cities. It was one of the earliest cities to
reach that scale through direct municipal merger, and it became Japan's
first city outside the Tokyo-Osaka-Nagoya corridor to reach designated-city
status, a reflection of how central the area's steel industry was to
Japan's postwar reconstruction.

### From Japan's first steel mill to a pollution crisis

The Yahata Steel Works, opened in 1901 as Japan's first fully integrated
modern steelworks, anchored what became one of Japan's most important
industrial zones for most of the 20th century — a role recognized when the
site was included in a 2015 UNESCO World Heritage listing covering Japan's
Meiji-era industrialization. That same concentration of heavy industry
came at a steep environmental cost: by the 1960s, Kitakyushu's Dokai Bay
was so polluted it was locally nicknamed the "Sea of Death," and the city
became one of Japan's starkest examples of industrial air and water
pollution during the country's rapid postwar growth.

### An environmental cleanup success story

What followed is now taught internationally as a genuine cleanup success:
sustained pressure from local citizens' groups (notably a coalition of
local women), combined with new pollution-control regulation and
substantial investment, brought measurable improvements to Kitakyushu's air
and water quality within roughly two decades. The city has since rebranded
around that recovery, building an "Eco-Town" cluster of recycling and
environmental-technology facilities on former industrial land and hosting
international delegations studying its pollution-recovery model, especially
from other rapidly industrializing countries.

## Getting there and getting around

Kokura Station is a Sanyo/Kyushu Shinkansen junction, about 5 hours from
Tokyo and just under an hour from Hakata (Fukuoka City); the underwater
Kanmon pedestrian tunnel and the Kanmon Bridge connect Kitakyushu directly
to Shimonoseki, Yamaguchi, across the strait. The city's five former towns
remain somewhat spread out, generally requiring train or bus travel between
areas like Mojiko Retro and central Kokura.
