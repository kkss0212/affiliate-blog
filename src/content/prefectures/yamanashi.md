Yamanashi holds Mount Fuji's northern face and the Fuji Five Lakes — the
side most people mean when they picture the classic postcard shot of the
mountain — while the summit itself is administratively split with
Shizuoka on the southern side, a geographic quirk that's shaped a
centuries-long, entirely friendly local rivalry over which prefecture the
mountain "really" belongs to.

### Yamanashi and the wider world

Yamanashi's wine industry has a direct, documented French origin story.
In 1877, early in the Meiji government's push to import Western
industries, two young men from Kofu, Tsuchiya Ryuken and Miyazaki
Masanari, were sent to study winemaking in France; they returned to found
Japan's first wine company in Yamanashi, using local Koshu grapes that had
been cultivated in the region since at least the 8th century. That
French-trained foundation is why Yamanashi, rather than any other
prefecture, became Japan's serious wine country — a rare case of Meiji-era
Western technology transfer (alongside Gunma's silk industry) that
produced a genuinely internationally competitive product rather than just
domestic industrialization.

### Neighbors: Yamanashi and Shizuoka

Mount Fuji's summit sits directly on the border between Yamanashi and
Shizuoka, and the two prefectures have maintained a long, mostly
good-humored rivalry over bragging rights to the mountain — including
competing claims about which side offers the better view, and periodic
half-serious debate over exactly whose mountain it is.

## Getting there and getting around

Kofu is on the JR Chuo Line, about 90 minutes from Tokyo by limited
express; the Fuji Five Lakes area is reached by direct highway bus from
Tokyo or by local train via Otsuki. A car is useful for the wineries
around Katsunuma.
