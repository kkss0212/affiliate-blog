Tokushima anchors the eastern edge of Shikoku, and much of what defines
it — a dye, a whirlpool, a festival — travels further than the
prefecture's modest size would suggest.

### Tokushima and the wider world

Tokushima's Awa-ai indigo dye became one of the more surprising Japanese
exports of ideas, if not always goods: when Western visitors arrived in
large numbers during the Meiji era, they were struck by how much of the
country's everyday clothing was dyed in the same deep blue, produced in
huge quantities in Tokushima. The British scientist Robert William
Atkinson, working in Japan in the late 1870s, is widely credited with
coining the term "Japan Blue" to describe it — a nickname that stuck in
English-language accounts of Japan for decades afterward.

More recently, the Naruto Strait's dramatic tidal whirlpools — among the
largest in the world, generated by the tidal flow between the Pacific
and the Seto Inland Sea — lent both their name and their swirling visual
pattern to "Naruto," one of the best-selling manga and anime franchises
globally; the whirlpool motif appears directly in the series' visual
branding, even though the story itself isn't set in Tokushima.

### Neighbors: Tokushima and the rest of Shikoku

Tokushima is where most walkers begin the Shikoku Henro, an 88-temple,
roughly 1,200-km pilgrimage circuit that loops through all four Shikoku
prefectures — a shared religious and cultural thread that ties the whole
island together more than any single rivalry between its prefectures.

## Getting there and getting around

Tokushima has no shinkansen connection; the fastest route from Osaka or
Kobe is by highway bus across the Akashi Kaikyo and Onaruto bridges,
roughly 2-2.5 hours. A car or bus is useful for reaching the Iya Valley.
