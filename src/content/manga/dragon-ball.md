Dragon Ball starts as something much smaller than what it became: a
martial-arts adventure comedy, loosely inspired by the classical Chinese
novel *Journey to the West*, following a monkey-tailed boy named Goku as he
travels the world collecting magical orbs. Akira Toriyama, born in Kiyosu,
Aichi Prefecture, drew and wrote the entire series solo across its
1984-1995 run in Weekly Shonen Jump — and over that decade, the story's
scope grew from village-level fights to battles that level entire planets,
without ever quite losing the earlier chapters' sense of humor.

### A Superman story, on purpose

Dragon Ball's most direct "wider world" connection isn't a licensing deal
or a festival appearance — it's baked into the plot itself. Toriyama was
open about being a fan of Western comics, and the Saiyan Saga's central
reveal (Goku is the last survivor of a destroyed alien warrior race, sent
to Earth as an infant, raised by an adoptive family, hiding immense
strength, ultimately fighting for the planet that took him in) is a
deliberate, well-documented homage to Superman's own origin story. It's
one of the clearer cases in manga history of an artist openly translating
a Western superhero mythology into a completely different visual and
narrative tradition.

### From Jump's golden age to global shorthand

Dragon Ball's domestic popularity through the late 1980s and early 1990s
helped push Weekly Shonen Jump to its all-time peak circulation of 6.53
million copies a week — a period fans and the industry both refer to as
the magazine's "Golden Age." That momentum carried the franchise well past
Japan: Toei Animation's multiple TV adaptations (Dragon Ball, Z, GT, and
Super) and 20-plus films built one of anime's most globally recognized
properties, to the point that Dragon Ball references now show up
routinely in Western sports celebrations, hip-hop lyrics, and internet
culture largely detached from the source material itself.

## Where to start

The original manga's first volume, "The Monkey King," is worth starting
with even if you only know the franchise from its later, more famous
Dragon Ball Z era — it establishes Goku's world before the story shifts
into the sci-fi power battles most casual fans picture first. If you'd
rather start with the anime, the original 1986 Dragon Ball series (not Z)
covers the same early material and is the more direct match for the
manga's tone.
