Wakayama occupies the western side of the Kii Peninsula, a mountainous,
sacred landscape that has drawn pilgrims for over a thousand years and
still functions, in parts, almost exactly as it did then.

### Wakayama and the wider world

Koyasan's founder, the monk Kukai, traveled to Tang-dynasty China in 804
to study esoteric Buddhism directly from Chinese masters, returning two
years later with the teachings that would become Shingon Buddhism —
Japan's own version of a tradition transmitted along the same broad
network of Buddhist exchange that connected China, Korea, and Japan for
centuries. Koyasan, which he founded in 816 as its headquarters, remains
active today, and its practice of shukubo — staying overnight in a
working temple, eating monastic vegetarian food (shojin ryori), and
optionally joining morning prayers — has become one of the specific
experiences most sought out by international, and especially Western,
visitors looking for something more immersive than a typical temple
visit.

### Neighbors: Wakayama, Mie, and Nara

Wakayama shares the Kumano Kodo pilgrimage network — twinned with Spain's
Camino de Santiago — with neighboring Mie and Nara, a rare case on this
site of three prefectures cooperating on a single UNESCO World Heritage
listing rather than competing over a shared feature.

## Getting there and getting around

Wakayama City is reached from Osaka in under an hour by direct train.
Koyasan requires a scenic cable car up from Gokurakubashi Station;
Shirahama and Nachi Falls are further south, best reached by limited
express train along the coast or by car.
