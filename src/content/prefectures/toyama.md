Toyama sits with the Japan Alps rising almost directly from its coastline
— a compressed geography that gives the prefecture both dramatic
mountains and a working fishing bay within a short drive of each other,
and shapes an economy built on farming what the mountains allow and
selling what its people have historically been good at making and moving.

### Toyama and the wider world

Toyama's most distinctive historical export wasn't a product so much as a
business model. Starting in the early 1700s, traveling salesmen known as
"Toyama no kusuriuri" fanned out across Japan carrying medicine chests
they'd leave in customers' homes, returning periodically to restock and
charge only for what had actually been used — an early trust-based
subscription system that built Toyama's reputation for commercial
integrity nationwide and, some historians argue, laid groundwork for
Japan's modern pharmaceutical industry, still a significant employer in
the prefecture today.

More recently, the Tateyama Kurobe Alpine Route has become one of
Toyama's most visible international draws, particularly popular with
Taiwanese visitors, who make up a large share of the route's foreign
tourism — the snow-wall corridor in particular has become a specific,
recognizable bucket-list image shared well beyond Japan.

### Neighbors: Toyama and the rest of Hokuriku

Toyama, Ishikawa, and Fukui are collectively known as the Hokuriku
region — three Sea of Japan-facing prefectures that share heavy winter
snowfall and a degree of historical distance from the Kyoto/Tokyo power
centers, even though Ishikawa's Kanazawa was itself one of the wealthiest
domains of the Edo period.

## Getting there and getting around

Toyama Station is a Hokuriku Shinkansen stop, about 2 hours from Tokyo.
The Tateyama Kurobe Alpine Route can be entered from the Toyama side or
crossed through to Nagano on the other end, making it a practical
mountain crossing as well as a tourist route.
