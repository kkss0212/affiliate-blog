Kobe opened as one of Japan's original treaty ports in 1868, later than
Yokohama and Nagasaki but under similar terms, and quickly developed its
own foreign settlement and merchant community around what's now Kitano-cho
and the harbor district. That century-and-a-half of sustained international
contact gave Kobe a reputation, still current today, as one of Japan's more
cosmopolitan and internationally comfortable cities — a self-image many
Kobe residents draw a clear line between and their more brash, distinctly
Japanese neighbor, Osaka, less than an hour away.

### January 17, 1995

Kobe's modern history is inescapably defined by the Great Hanshin
earthquake, a magnitude 6.9 quake that struck before dawn on January 17,
1995, killing more than 6,000 people and destroying or badly damaging tens
of thousands of buildings across the city — at the time, Japan's deadliest
earthquake since 1923. The disaster exposed serious gaps in Japan's
building codes and disaster-response coordination, and the lessons drawn
from Kobe's experience directly shaped stronger seismic building standards
and emergency-response reforms adopted nationwide in the years that
followed, including ahead of the 2011 Tohoku earthquake.

Kobe's recovery over the following decade — rebuilding its port, its
neighborhoods, and its economy largely from the ground up — is now told
locally as much as a story of civic resilience as of the disaster itself,
and the rebuilt Harborland waterfront and Port Tower area stand as the
clearest physical symbol of that recovery today.

### A narrow city between mountains and sea

Kobe's geography — a thin developed strip squeezed between the Rokko
mountain range and Osaka Bay — gives it an unusually compact, walkable
urban form for a city of its size, with hillside neighborhoods like Kitano-
cho sitting only a short walk from the harbor. That same narrow footprint,
though, was part of what made the 1995 earthquake so devastating, with
almost no part of the developed city far from the affected area.

## Getting there and getting around

Sannomiya and Kobe Stations are about 30 minutes from Osaka and 15 minutes
from Kyoto (via Shin-Kobe's Shinkansen stop, roughly 3 hours from Tokyo).
The compact city center is easily walkable, with the Nunobiki Ropeway and
local buses covering the surrounding hills.
