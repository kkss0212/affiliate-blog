Hyogo holds two of Kansai's most distinct identities in one prefecture:
Himeji, home to the single most photographed and best-preserved castle in
Japan, and Kobe, a port city whose character was shaped almost entirely
by 150 years of contact with the outside world.

### Hyogo and the wider world

Kobe opened as one of Japan's later treaty ports in 1868, and quickly
developed a substantial foreign merchant community on the hillside above
the harbor, in a district now called Kitano-cho — dozens of Western-style
houses built by British, American, and other foreign residents survive
there today, several open as small museums, giving Kobe a genuinely
different visual and cultural texture from most Japanese cities.

Hyogo's more recent, much harder international moment came on January
17, 1995, when the Great Hanshin earthquake struck Kobe directly, killing
over 6,400 people and destroying large parts of the city, including
sections of elevated highway that collapsed on live television. The
rebuilding that followed — new building codes, retrofitted infrastructure,
and a redeveloped waterfront — has since been studied internationally as
a model for urban earthquake recovery, and Kobe today shows few visible
scars of the disaster.

### Neighbors: Hyogo and the rest of Kansai

Kobe forms the western point of the Keihanshin metropolitan triangle with
Osaka and Kyoto, generally positioned as the region's international port
city rather than a direct rival to either — a role distinct from, but
complementary to, its two more historically dominant neighbors.

## Getting there and getting around

Shin-Kobe Station is a Tokaido/Sanyo Shinkansen stop, about 3 hours from
Tokyo and 15 minutes from Osaka. Himeji Station is further west on the
same Shinkansen line, roughly 50 minutes from Shin-Kobe.
