Ehime occupies Shikoku's northwestern corner, and its best-known
attractions span an unusually wide range — an ancient hot spring, a
century-old novel, and a 21st-century cycling route that draws an
entirely different kind of visitor than either.

### Ehime and the wider world

The Shimanami Kaido is Ehime's clearest modern connection to the
international travel world: since opening in 2006 for cyclists (the
final link in a longer expressway that had been built in stages since
the 1970s), the route's combination of dedicated cycling lanes, six
distinct bridge crossings, and small-island scenery has drawn repeated
recognition from international cycling publications and outlets like
CNN as one of the best cycling routes anywhere, pulling in cycling
tourists from well beyond Japan specifically to ride it.

Matsuyama's literary connection runs in a different direction: Natsume
Soseki, who would go on to become one of modern Japan's most important
novelists (his face appeared on the 1,000-yen note for two decades), spent
a year teaching English in Matsuyama in the late 1890s. He turned the
experience into "Botchan," a wry, still widely read 1906 novel about a
brash young Tokyo teacher clashing with rural Matsuyama — the book is
now so closely associated with the city that a Botchan-branded steam
locomotive still runs through central Matsuyama as a tourist attraction.

### Neighbors: Ehime and the rest of Shikoku

Ehime shares the Shikoku Henro pilgrimage circuit with its three island
neighbors, and its Shimanami Kaido physically ties it to Hiroshima's
Onomichi across the water — one of the few direct physical links between
Shikoku and Honshu that doesn't run through Kagawa.

## Getting there and getting around

Matsuyama Airport has domestic flights from major cities; Matsuyama
Station is reached from Okayama by limited express train in about 2.5-3
hours. A rental bicycle (with drop-off points along the route) is the
standard way to do the Shimanami Kaido.
