Ibaraki sits directly northeast of Tokyo, close enough that its southern
edge is effectively commuter territory — and yet it's become a genuine
national punchline in Japan's annual prefectural "attractiveness" survey,
a piece of self-deprecating trivia locals have mostly leaned into rather
than fought. It's also, less jokingly, home to a serious concentration of
Japanese science and industry.

### Ibaraki and the wider world

Starting in the 1960s, the Japanese government built Tsukuba Science
City from farmland in southern Ibaraki as a deliberate, planned
concentration of national research institutions — it now hosts JAXA's
main space center (mission control for Japan's astronauts and satellite
launches), the University of Tsukuba, and dozens of government and
corporate research labs, giving the prefecture an unusually dense, active
set of ongoing international scientific collaborations for its size.

Ibaraki's older global connection runs through industry: Hitachi, now one
of the world's largest electronics and industrial conglomerates, traces
its name and origin directly to the town of Hitachi, where it was founded
in 1910 repairing electric motors for a local copper mine before
expanding into the sprawling multinational it is today.

### Neighbors: Ibaraki and Tokyo

Ibaraki's defining relationship is with Tokyo itself — close enough to be
economically tied to the capital, but culturally positioned (fairly or
not) as its unglamorous rural cousin, a status shared to varying degrees
by several of Tokyo's other outer Kanto neighbors like Ibaraki, Tochigi,
and Gunma.

## Getting there and getting around

Mito Station is reachable from Tokyo's Ueno Station in about 70-90
minutes by limited express train; Tsukuba is connected to central Tokyo
by the Tsukuba Express line in about 45 minutes. A rental car helps for
Hitachi Seaside Park and Fukuroda Falls.
