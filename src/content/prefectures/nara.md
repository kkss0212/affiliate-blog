Before Kyoto, there was Nara. In 710, the imperial court established
Japan's first permanent capital at Heijo-kyo, on the site of modern Nara
City, ending centuries of the practice of moving the capital with each
new emperor. For 74 years, Nara was the political and religious center of
Japan — the period that gave the era its name, and produced some of the
country's oldest and most significant Buddhist art and architecture,
before the court relocated first briefly to Nagaoka and then, in 794, to
Kyoto.

### Nara and the wider world

Nara's international reach during that period was genuinely remarkable.
The city functioned as the eastern terminus of the Silk Road, and Todai-
ji's Shosoin Repository — an imperial storehouse built in the 8th century
— still holds thousands of objects donated by Emperor Shomu, including
glassware, textiles, and musical instruments whose origins trace back
through Tang-dynasty China to Persia, India, and Central Asia. When
Todai-ji's Great Buddha was completed in 752, its "eye-opening" dedication
ceremony was reportedly attended by monks and envoys from as far as India
and the Silk Road states — an extraordinary gathering for the 8th century,
and a strong indication of how connected Nara-period Japan already was to
the rest of Asia.

### Neighbors: Nara and Kyoto

Nara's relationship with Kyoto follows a pattern that repeats across
Japanese history: an older capital ceding formal power to a newer one
while retaining outsized cultural and religious weight — the same
dynamic that would later play out between Kyoto and Tokyo after 1868.

## Getting there and getting around

Nara is reached from Kyoto or Osaka by direct train in 35-50 minutes;
there's no shinkansen stop in Nara itself. Central Nara, including Todai-
ji, Nara Park, and Kasuga Taisha, is compact enough to cover on foot.
