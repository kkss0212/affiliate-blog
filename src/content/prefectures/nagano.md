Nagano is landlocked and mountainous enough to border eight other
prefectures — Niigata, Gunma, Saitama, Yamanashi, Shizuoka, Aichi, Gifu,
and Toyama — more neighbors than all but a couple of other prefectures in
the country, a direct consequence of sitting at the geographic center of
Japan's central mountain spine.

### Nagano and the wider world

Nagano's clearest, most concentrated moment on the world stage was the
1998 Winter Olympics, which brought new international-standard sports
venues, a purpose-built shinkansen line from Tokyo, and weeks of global
television coverage to a prefecture that had, until then, been known
domestically for mountains and soba rather than internationally for
anything in particular. The infrastructure built for the Games — venues,
the rail line, hotel capacity — still underpins a lot of how visitors
reach and move through Nagano today.

Zenko-ji Temple represents a much older kind of international-adjacent
significance: founded more than 1,400 years ago, before Japanese
Buddhism split into the various sects (Zen, Pure Land, Nichiren, and so
on) that define most other Japanese temples' affiliations today, it has
remained deliberately non-denominational — a genuinely unusual position
that has made it a pilgrimage site across sectarian lines for well over a
millennium.

### Neighbors: Nagano and the rest of Chubu

Bordering eight prefectures means Nagano doesn't have one single defining
rival the way some smaller prefectures do — its relationships shift by
region, from ski-culture overlap with Niigata and Gunma in the north to a
shared claim on parts of the Japan Alps with Gifu and Toyama to the west.

## Getting there and getting around

Nagano Station is a Hokuriku Shinkansen stop, about 90 minutes from
Tokyo. Matsumoto and the Japan Alps areas are reached by local train or
limited express from Nagano City; Kamikochi requires switching to a
dedicated shuttle bus, as private cars aren't permitted into the valley.
