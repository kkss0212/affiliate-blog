Fullmetal Alchemist's central rule of magic — alchemy, in this world,
requires "Equivalent Exchange": you cannot gain something without giving
up something of equal value — sounds like a tidy fantasy-worldbuilding
device on its own. It reads as something more grounded once you know
Hiromu Arakawa grew up on her family's dairy farm in what's now Makubetsu,
Hokkaido, a background she's cited directly as the inspiration for that
theme. Serialized in Monthly Shonen Gangan from 2001 to 2010, the series
follows brothers Edward and Alphonse Elric, whose own catastrophic
alchemy experiment (attempting to resurrect their dead mother) costs
Edward a limb and Alphonse his entire body — a literalized opening
statement of exactly what the story's central rule means in practice.

### Two anime, and which one to actually watch

Fullmetal Alchemist has an unusually well-known adaptation quirk: its
first anime, in 2003, ran out of source material partway through
production and diverged into its own original ending, while a second
adaptation, Fullmetal Alchemist: Brotherhood (2009), followed the manga's
actual story faithfully once enough of it existed. Brotherhood is
generally the recommended entry point today and is frequently ranked among
the highest-rated anime ever made on aggregator sites, but the original
2003 series still has its own defenders and a genuinely different (not
simply "wrong") ending worth watching on its own terms.

### An artist's farm upbringing, drawn directly into the theme

It's rare for a manga artist's personal biography to map this cleanly onto
their work's central idea. Arakawa has spoken about her Hokkaido dairy-farm
upbringing — including, by her own account, slaughtering and processing
livestock as part of farm life — as the direct source of the series'
insistence that nothing of value comes free, that every gain has a real,
often uncomfortable cost. It's a rare case where knowing the creator's
background genuinely changes how a fictional magic system reads.

## Where to start

Volume 1 is the right place to begin, and if you want the anime version,
go straight to Fullmetal Alchemist: Brotherhood (2009) rather than the
original 2003 series — it follows the manga's actual story rather than
branching into its own separate ending partway through.
