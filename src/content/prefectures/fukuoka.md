Fukuoka's Hakata Bay has been Japan's front door to the Asian continent for
longer than almost anywhere else in the country — closer to Busan, South
Korea, than to Tokyo, and historically the first stop for envoys, traders,
and, twice, invading fleets.

### Fukuoka and the outside world

In 1274 and again in 1281, the Mongol ruler Kublai Khan sent invasion
fleets against Japan, landing first at Hakata Bay. Samurai defenders held
the beaches behind hastily built stone walls, but both invasions were
ultimately broken up by typhoons — remembered in Japanese as kamikaze,
"divine wind," a term the WWII-era suicide attack units deliberately
borrowed centuries later for its providential connotation. Fragments of the
defensive wall are still visible in parts of Fukuoka today.

That gateway role never really stopped: Fukuoka became a major hub for
trade and diplomatic missions with Korea and China throughout Japanese
history, and remains, today, one of the closest major Japanese cities to
the Asian mainland — closer to Seoul than to Tokyo, with a direct
high-speed ferry to Busan.

### Neighbors: Fukuoka and the rest of Kyushu

Fukuoka is Kyushu's largest and most economically dominant city by a wide
margin, which gives it a slightly different relationship with the rest of
the island than, say, Osaka has with Kyoto — less a rivalry between equals,
more a regional capital that other Kyushu prefectures (like nearby
Nagasaki and Kumamoto) measure themselves against.

## Getting there and getting around

Fukuoka Airport is unusually central — a subway ride of about 10 minutes
from Hakata Station — making it one of the most convenient major airports
in Japan. Hakata Station is also a shinkansen terminus, putting Osaka
about 2.5 hours away by rail.
