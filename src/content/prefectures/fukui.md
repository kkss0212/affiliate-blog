Fukui rounds out the Hokuriku region's southern edge, and carries two
reputations that have little to do with each other: it's Japan's center
of dinosaur paleontology, and it's also one of the country's most
consistently well-regarded places to actually live.

### Fukui and the wider world

Fukui's deepest international influence runs through religion rather
than tourism. Eiheiji, founded in 1244 by the monk Dogen, became one of
the two head temples of Soto Zen Buddhism — a lineage that, transmitted
through later teachers over the following centuries, eventually reached
the West directly: Shunryu Suzuki, a Soto Zen priest trained in this same
tradition, moved to San Francisco in 1959 and founded the San Francisco
Zen Center, becoming one of the most influential figures in bringing Zen
practice to American audiences. Eiheiji itself, still an active training
monastery today, is one genuine root of what a great many Westerners now
recognize as "Zen."

More recently, Fukui's dinosaur fossil beds — among the richest in Asia —
have drawn ongoing joint research expeditions with paleontologists from
Canada, Mongolia, and elsewhere, and the resulting Fukui Prefectural
Dinosaur Museum has become an internationally recognized research
institution in its own right, not just a tourist attraction.

### Neighbors: Fukui and the rest of Hokuriku

Fukui is the southernmost of the three Hokuriku prefectures (with Toyama
and Ishikawa), and its history is somewhat more entangled with the Kansai
region to its south than its northern Hokuriku neighbors — Fukui's
southern Wakasa area historically supplied Kyoto with seafood via routes
still nicknamed the "Saba (Mackerel) Kaido."

## Getting there and getting around

Fukui Station is a Hokuriku Shinkansen stop as of the line's 2024
extension, cutting travel time from Tokyo to roughly 3 hours. Eiheiji and
Tojinbo are both reachable by local train or bus from Fukui City.
