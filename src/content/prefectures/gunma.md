Gunma is landlocked and mountainous, which historically pushed its economy
toward two very different things: sericulture (silk farming) in its
foothills and valleys, and the hot springs produced by its volcanic
geology — both of which still define the prefecture today.

### Gunma and the wider world

Tomioka Silk Mill is Gunma's clearest connection to the outside world,
and one of the more concrete examples anywhere in Japan of the Meiji
government's deliberate strategy of importing Western technology
wholesale. Built in 1872, the mill was designed by French civil engineer
Paul Brunat, equipped with French machinery, and initially staffed in
part by French instructors training Japanese workers in industrial-scale
silk reeling — a single factory that helped turn silk into Japan's leading
export and financed much of the country's early industrialization. It's
now preserved as a UNESCO World Heritage Site specifically for
demonstrating that technology transfer.

Gunma's other notable export is less industrial and more personal: the
prefecture has produced a disproportionate number of Japan's postwar
prime ministers, most notably two generations of the Fukuda family —
enough of a pattern that it's sometimes only half-jokingly called Japan's
"kingdom of prime ministers."

### Neighbors: Gunma and the rest of Kita-Kanto

Gunma rounds out the informal "Kita-Kanto" (North Kanto) trio with
Ibaraki and Tochigi — inland, mountainous, and generally overshadowed in
national attention by Tokyo immediately to the south, despite each
holding at least one genuinely world-class attraction.

## Getting there and getting around

Takasaki Station is a Hokuriku/Joetsu Shinkansen stop, about an hour from
Tokyo; Kusatsu Onsen requires a further bus connection. A rental car
opens up Gunma's mountain roads and less centrally located onsen towns.
