Kyoto City's deep history as Japan's imperial capital for over a thousand
years is covered on the Kyoto prefecture page — this page instead looks at
the practical, present-day challenge of actually running a city whose
historic core has become one of the most sought-after tourist destinations
on Earth.

### A city built to a human scale, now visited at industrial scale

Kyoto's historic urban form — narrow lanes, low wooden buildings, a compact
network of temples and shrines interwoven with ordinary residential streets
— was never designed to absorb tens of millions of annual visitors. As
inbound tourism to Japan grew sharply through the 2010s, Kyoto became one of
the most visible global case studies in "overtourism": narrow lanes in
neighborhoods like Gion regularly overwhelmed by day-trip crowds, city buses
so packed that some routes became impractical for residents actually trying
to commute, and long-simmering tension between the tourism-dependent local
economy and the quality of life of people who actually live there.

City hall's response has included a 2023 decision to sharply raise entrance
fees at several of the city's most-visited sites, dedicated tourist bus
routes separated from regular city services, restrictions on photography in
parts of Gion after repeated incidents of visitors harassing maiko and
geiko, and some of Japan's strictest urban design regulations more broadly —
building-height caps, restrictions on rooftop signage, and requirements
that even convenience-store signage use muted colors, all aimed at
protecting the historic cityscape from the kind of visual clutter common
elsewhere in urban Japan.

### Balancing preservation and a living city

Kyoto's UNESCO status — 17 separate sites collectively inscribed as the
Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto — makes the city's preservation
obligations unusually formal and far-reaching for a municipal government of
its size, and city planners have to weigh those obligations against the
practical needs of a modern city of 1.5 million residents whose own
population has been gently declining even as visitor numbers climbed.

## Getting there and getting around

Kyoto Station is a Tokaido Shinkansen stop, about 2 hours 15 minutes from
Tokyo and 15 minutes from Osaka. The city center is served by a small
subway network, an extensive bus system (increasingly supplemented by
dedicated tourist routes to ease congestion on regular lines), and is
genuinely pleasant to cover by rental bicycle outside peak summer heat.
