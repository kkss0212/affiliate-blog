Hatsune Miku isn't a musician in any conventional sense — she's a
singing-voice-synthesis product, released in August 2007 by Sapporo-based
software company Crypton Future Media, built on Yamaha's Vocaloid2 engine
and voiced by actress Saki Fujita. Buy the software, type in lyrics and a
melody, and it sings in her voice. That's the entire premise, and on paper
it sounds like a niche production tool rather than the foundation of an
international touring act — which makes what happened next genuinely
unusual.

### A phenomenon built by tens of thousands of strangers

Crypton never wrote a single Hatsune Miku song themselves. Instead, an
enormous, decentralized community of independent "Vocaloid producers"
picked up the software and started releasing music under her voice — over
100,000 songs at this point, by some counts, with thousands sold
commercially through Crypton's own Karent distribution label. The 2009
album by producer duo supercell, featuring tracks like "Melt" and "World is
Mine," is usually credited as the release that took Miku from a niche
internet phenomenon to a mainstream J-Pop presence, and that
producer-driven model — an open character voiced by whoever wants to use
her — has stayed the format ever since.

### From a Sapporo office to Coachella

The most striking part of Miku's international spread is how literally she
travels: her touring concerts ("Hatsune Miku Expo" among others) use
synchronized projection to put her on stage in North America, Europe, and
Asia, performing alongside a live backing band of human musicians. That
format has taken her as far as Coachella, one of the more unusual festival
bookings in recent memory — a projected, non-human "performer" sharing a
bill with conventional touring acts, headquartered the entire time out of
a software company's office in Sapporo, Hokkaido.

## Where to start

The supercell album (2009), featuring "Melt" and "World is Mine," is the
clearest entry point — it's the release most responsible for taking Miku
beyond the Vocaloid-producer community and into mainstream J-Pop
listening. From there, footage of her live concert format (widely
available on video platforms) makes the case for why she's worth
understanding as a live phenomenon, not just a studio one — a character
with no physical existence, filling arenas anyway.
