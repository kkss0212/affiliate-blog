Kanazawa was, by Edo-period standards, an unusually rich domain that
deliberately avoided looking like a threat to the shogunate — the ruling
Maeda clan reportedly poured resources into gardens, crafts, tea ceremony,
and the arts rather than military buildup, a strategy that kept them
wealthy and largely undisturbed for over two centuries. That same
instinct arguably saved the city again in the 20th century: Kanazawa
avoided the heavy Allied bombing that leveled most of Japan's other major
castle towns, leaving its historic districts intact in a way few Japanese
cities can match today.

### Ishikawa and the wider world

That preserved craft culture has become Kanazawa's most concrete modern
connection to the outside world. The city was designated a UNESCO
Creative City of Crafts and Folk Art in 2009, and its gold leaf — nearly
all of Japan's total output — has found its way into international luxury
goods: gold-leaf-flecked sake, desserts, and cosmetics sold well beyond
Japan often trace back to workshops here.

Ishikawa's Noto Peninsula, the prefecture's northern arm, was struck by a
major earthquake on January 1, 2024, causing significant damage and loss
of life in fishing and farming communities along the coast. Recovery has
been ongoing since; check current official guidance before planning
travel specifically to the Noto Peninsula, as conditions continue to
change.

### Neighbors: Ishikawa and the rest of Hokuriku

Ishikawa sits between Toyama and Fukui as the middle prefecture of the
Hokuriku region, and its capital, Kanazawa, is by far the region's
largest and most historically significant city — a status Toyama and
Fukui's own capitals don't quite match.

## Getting there and getting around

Kanazawa Station is a Hokuriku Shinkansen terminus, about 2.5 hours from
Tokyo. The city center is walkable or served by a loop bus; a car is more
useful for reaching the Noto Peninsula.
