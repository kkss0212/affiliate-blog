Sendai exists because of one man: Date Masamune, a regional lord who lost
an eye to smallpox as a child (earning him the nickname "Dokuganryu," the
One-Eyed Dragon) and built Sendai as his castle town in the early 1600s
after backing the winning side in the wars that founded the Tokugawa
shogunate. Everything about how Miyagi presents itself today — the tree
cover, the castle ruins, the mausoleum, the annual Date-themed festivals —
traces back to him.

### Miyagi and the wider world

Date Masamune's most surprising legacy is diplomatic: in 1613, he sent a
delegation led by his retainer Hasekura Tsunenaga across the Pacific to
Mexico, then on to Spain and finally Rome, where Hasekura met Pope Paul V
— making it Japan's first direct diplomatic mission to Europe, seven years
before Sakoku (the closed-country policy) would have made such a voyage
impossible. The mission sought a trade and missionary agreement with
Spain; it largely failed, and by the time Hasekura returned to Japan seven
years later, the political climate toward Christianity had turned hostile.
Hasekura's own descendants and several crew members are believed to have
stayed behind in Spain, and a small Spanish town, Coria del Rio, still has
residents with the surname Japon today, reportedly tracing back to them.

### Neighbors: Miyagi and the rest of Tohoku

Sendai is unambiguously Tohoku's biggest city and functions as its
de facto regional capital — the hub other Tohoku prefectures (Iwate,
Akita, Yamagata) route through for anything that needs a bigger city than
their own capitals offer, closer in relationship to Fukuoka-and-Kyushu
than to any classic rivalry.

## Getting there and getting around

Sendai Station is a Tohoku Shinkansen stop, about 1.5 hours from Tokyo,
and Sendai Airport has domestic and limited international connections.
Matsushima and Yamadera are both easy half-day trips from Sendai by local
train.
