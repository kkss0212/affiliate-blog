Akita faces the Sea of Japan rather than the Pacific, which historically
put it on a different trade and travel route than Tohoku's eastern
prefectures — closer, in some periods, to shipping routes running up to
Hokkaido and across to the Asian mainland than to Tokyo overland.

### Akita and the wider world

Akita's best-known international export has four legs. The Akita Inu, a
sturdy spitz-type dog bred here for centuries as a hunting and guard dog,
became a global name through Hachiko — an Akita who continued waiting at
Tokyo's Shibuya Station for his deceased owner every day for nearly a
decade in the 1920s-30s, a story that inspired statues, a Japanese film,
and the 2009 Hollywood film "Hachi: A Dog's Tale" starring Richard Gere.
The breed's American profile was independently boosted even earlier: in
1937, Helen Keller was given an Akita puppy named Kamikaze-go during a
visit to Japan — when he died of distemper shortly after, the Japanese
government sent her another, Kenzan-go, and Keller is widely credited as
the person who first brought the breed to the United States.

### Neighbors: Akita and the rest of Tohoku

Akita's Sea of Japan coastline sets it apart from Pacific-facing Iwate and
Miyagi, both geographically and in how directly it was affected by the
2011 tsunami (comparatively little, versus the devastating impact on the
Pacific coast). Within Tohoku's shared festival culture, Akita's Kanto
Festival is grouped with Aomori's Nebuta and Sendai's Tanabata as the
region's "three great festivals."

## Getting there and getting around

Akita Airport and Akita Station (a Shinkansen terminus via the Akita
Shinkansen line) are the main gateways, roughly 4 hours from Tokyo by
rail. Kakunodate and Lake Tazawa are both reachable by local train, though
a rental car opens up the more remote Oga Peninsula and onsen areas.
