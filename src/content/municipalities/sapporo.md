Sapporo barely existed before the Meiji government decided it needed to. In
1868, the new government moved to formally settle and develop Hokkaido — until
then home mostly to the indigenous Ainu people and a scattering of Japanese
trading posts — partly to secure the northern frontier against Russian
expansion. Sapporo was chosen as the new Hokkaido Development Commission's
headquarters in 1869 and laid out from scratch on a wide, regular grid, a
striking departure from the organic street plans of Japan's older cities.

### An American-designed university, an Asian first, a beer

The Meiji government leaned heavily on foreign advisors to develop Hokkaido's
unfamiliar northern climate and terrain, and Sapporo's most enduring
institutional legacy came from one of them: Massachusetts agricultural
scientist William S. Clark, who spent less than a year in 1876 setting up what
became Hokkaido University, but whose farewell line to his students — "Boys,
be ambitious" — is still widely quoted in Japan today. That same year, the
government opened the Kaitakushi Brewery, the direct ancestor of Sapporo
Beer, using German brewing techniques adapted to Hokkaido's cold climate and
barley.

Sapporo's clearest moment on the world stage came in 1972, when it hosted the
Winter Olympics — the first Winter Games held anywhere in Asia. The Games
left the city with much of its modern transit infrastructure, including its
subway system, and cemented Sapporo's identity as a winter-sports and
winter-festival city, an identity the Sapporo Snow Festival (first held in
1950, well before the Olympics) had already begun building.

### Sapporo and the rest of Hokkaido

As Hokkaido's prefectural capital, Sapporo functions as the administrative,
economic, and transit hub for an island more than twice the size of Kyushu —
most travelers heading anywhere else in Hokkaido pass through New Chitose
Airport and Sapporo Station first. The city's rapid postwar growth (it didn't
pass a million residents until 1970) has made it something of an outlier
within Hokkaido, whose smaller towns have mostly been shrinking for decades.

## Getting there and getting around

New Chitose Airport, about 40 minutes south of the city center by rapid
train, is Hokkaido's main air gateway and well connected to international
routes across East Asia. Sapporo itself has a small subway network plus
streetcars and buses; the city center's grid layout makes it unusually easy
to navigate on foot compared with most Japanese cities.
