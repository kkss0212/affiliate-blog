Tokyo has only been Japan's capital since 1868 — younger, in that job, than the
United States is as a country. For over a thousand years before that it was a
regional castle town called Edo, important but not the seat of imperial
power; when the shogunate that ran the country from Edo Castle collapsed and
the emperor moved from Kyoto, the city was renamed Tokyo ("Eastern Capital")
and grew, in roughly 150 years, into the largest metropolitan area on Earth.

### Tokyo and the wider world

Tokyo's most consequential brush with the West happened just offshore: in
1853, US Commodore Matthew Perry's "Black Ships" anchored near Uraga, at the
mouth of Tokyo Bay, and effectively forced the end of Japan's 220-year
policy of closed borders. Fifteen years later the Meiji Restoration
followed, and the new government spent the next few decades deliberately
importing Western institutions — universities, a constitution, a rail
network — into the newly renamed capital.

The 20th century brought heavier contact still. Tokyo was firebombed
extensively in 1945, then served as the headquarters of the Allied
occupation (1945-1952) under General Douglas MacArthur, based in what's now
the Dai-Ichi Life building facing the Imperial Palace. Less than two decades
later, the 1964 Summer Olympics — the first held in Asia — were staged
partly as a statement that the postwar rebuild was complete; the 2020 Games
(held in 2021, delayed by the pandemic) made Tokyo the first city to host
the Summer Olympics twice outside Europe and North America.

### Neighbors: Tokyo, Osaka, and everywhere else

Tokyo and Osaka anchor Japan's classic east-west rivalry — Tokyo cast as
buttoned-up and status-conscious, Osaka as loud and funny — but Tokyo's more
practical relationship is with the prefectures physically fused to it:
Kanagawa (Yokohama), Saitama, and Chiba together form the Greater Tokyo
Area, commonly cited as the world's most populous metropolitan region at
somewhere around 37-38 million people, depending on where the boundary is
drawn.

## Getting there and getting around

Narita and Haneda are Tokyo's two international airports (Haneda is
considerably closer to central Tokyo); the shinkansen network radiates out
from Tokyo Station in every direction. Within the city, the JR Yamanote
loop line and an extensive subway network make a car genuinely unnecessary
— a rarity for a city this size.
