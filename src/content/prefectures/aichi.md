Aichi is routinely described, alongside Tokyo and Osaka, as one of
Japan's three major metropolitan economic centers — a status built almost
entirely on manufacturing, with Nagoya as its capital and Toyota as its
single most consequential company.

### Aichi and the wider world

Toyota's global influence runs deeper than car sales. The Toyota
Production System, developed at the company's Aichi plants over decades
starting in the mid-20th century, introduced concepts like just-in-time
inventory and kaizen (continuous incremental improvement) that were
eventually studied, adopted, and renamed "lean manufacturing" by
industries far outside automaking, worldwide — a rare case of a specific
regional company's internal management philosophy becoming a globally
taught business school subject in its own right.

Aichi's older claim to significance is religious rather than industrial:
Atsuta Shrine is said to house Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi, one of the three
sacred Imperial Regalia (alongside a mirror and a jewel) said to have
been passed down since Japan's mythical founding and used in every
imperial enthronement — though, in keeping with tradition, the sword
itself has never been displayed publicly or verified to exist by anyone
outside the imperial household and shrine priests.

### Neighbors: Aichi, Tokyo, and Osaka

Nagoya's defining relationship is a three-way one: it's consistently
ranked as Japan's third major metropolitan center behind Tokyo and Osaka,
close enough to both by shinkansen (roughly 100 minutes and 50 minutes,
respectively) to draw comparisons, but distinct enough in character and
economy — manufacturing rather than finance or historic capital status —
to avoid feeling like an also-ran to either.

## Getting there and getting around

Nagoya Station is a major Tokaido Shinkansen hub, about 100 minutes from
Tokyo and under an hour from Kyoto or Osaka, making it a practical
stopover point for travelers moving between the two. Central Nagoya is
well served by subway.
