Kumamoto Castle, begun in 1601 by the daimyo and renowned castle engineer
Kato Kiyomasa, was built with a reputation for near-impregnability that
would be tested for real nearly three centuries later. During the 1877
Satsuma Rebellion, the last and largest of the samurai uprisings against the
new Meiji government, forces loyal to the legendary Saigo Takamori laid
siege to Kumamoto Castle for about seven weeks. The garrison, though badly
outnumbered, held out — a defense credited partly to Kato Kiyomasa's
original design decisions, made nearly 300 years earlier — buying time for
government reinforcements to arrive and ultimately break the rebellion.
Kiyomasa himself, remembered locally for both his castle-building skill and
practical flood-control and land-reclamation projects around the city, is
still venerated at Kato Shrine within the castle grounds.

### A mascot that outgrew its marketing campaign

Kumamoto's most unexpected modern export has nothing to do with castles.
Kumamon, a wide-eyed black bear character created in 2011 to promote the
Kyushu Shinkansen's extension to Kumamoto, was designed by a well-known
Japanese art director and quickly outgrew its original marketing brief,
becoming licensed on an enormous range of products and, by some measures,
one of the most commercially valuable regional mascots ever produced in
Japan's crowded yuru-kyara (mascot character) market — a genuine case study
in how a local tourism campaign can become a national phenomenon almost by
accident.

### Rebuilding after 2016

In April 2016, a pair of major earthquakes struck the Kumamoto region,
severely damaging Kumamoto Castle — including collapsed stone walls and a
partially destroyed main tower roof — along with large parts of the city
and surrounding area. Restoration of the castle has proceeded in stages
over the following years, with the main keep reopened to visitors well
before some of the more intricate stonework repairs are complete, making
an ongoing, visible restoration project part of what visitors see today.

## Getting there and getting around

Kumamoto Station is a Kyushu Shinkansen stop, about 4 hours from Tokyo and
40 minutes from Hakata (Fukuoka City). The castle and Suizenji Jojuen are
both reachable by the city's streetcar network, which covers most central
sightseeing areas.
