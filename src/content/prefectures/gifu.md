Gifu splits neatly into two regions that don't always feel like the same
prefecture: mountainous Hida in the north, home to Shirakawa-go's snow-
country villages and the old town of Takayama, and flatter Mino in the
south, historically the seat of real political power and today closely
tied to nearby Nagoya's economy.

### Gifu and the wider world

That political power ran through Gifu City itself. In 1567, the warlord
Oda Nobunaga — one of the three men credited with unifying Japan out of
over a century of civil war — captured the castle town then called
Inokuchi, renamed it Gifu, and ruled from its hilltop castle as he began
the military campaigns that would eventually put most of Japan under
central authority for the first time in generations. Nobunaga was also
unusually receptive, by the standards of his era, to the Portuguese
Jesuit missionaries and traders then arriving in Japan, welcoming their
presence and adopting European firearms technology that reshaped how
Japanese warfare was fought going forward — an early, consequential point
of contact between a powerful Japanese ruler and the West.

### Neighbors: Gifu and the rest of Chubu

Gifu's internal Hida/Mino split extends outward into its regional
relationships: mountainous Hida shares more culturally with neighboring
Toyama and the Japan Alps prefectures, while Mino's economy runs largely
on its proximity to Aichi and the industrial Nagoya region just south of
it.

## Getting there and getting around

Gifu-Hashima Station is a Tokaido Shinkansen stop, and Nagoya (a 20-minute
train ride away) is the more common gateway for most visitors. Takayama
and Shirakawa-go are reached by highway bus from either Takayama Station
or Nagoya, as neither is directly served by rail.
