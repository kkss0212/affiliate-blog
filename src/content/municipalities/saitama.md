Saitama City is, administratively speaking, a young invention: it was
created in 2001 by merging the older cities of Urawa, Omiya, and Yono
(joined in 2005 by Iwatsuki), a deliberate consolidation aimed at giving
Saitama Prefecture a capital city large enough to match its population —
before the merger, the prefecture was unusual in having no single dominant
city of its own. The result took the prefecture's own name for its
municipal name, an arrangement Japan doesn't repeat often (Fukushima,
Yamagata, and a handful of others being similar exceptions).

### A railway town before it was a capital

Long before the 2001 merger, Omiya had already built a distinct identity as
one of Japan's most important railway hubs: chosen in the 1880s as a
junction and maintenance-workshop site partly for its position and partly
through local lobbying, Omiya grew around the rail industry in a way that
still shapes the area today, from its large marshalling yards to the
Railway Museum, one of Japan's most comprehensive collections of preserved
rolling stock. That railway heritage sits alongside an unrelated but
equally distinctive local tradition: Omiya Bonsai Village, founded in the
1920s after a group of Tokyo bonsai growers relocated here (partly following
the 1923 Great Kanto Earthquake) in search of suitable soil and water,
building a bonsai-cultivation reputation that draws visitors and collectors
internationally.

### Saitama within greater Tokyo

As the literal namesake capital of Saitama Prefecture, the city functions
primarily as an administrative and commuter hub within the vast Tokyo
metropolitan area rather than as an independent tourist draw — most
residents commute into central Tokyo for work, a pattern the 2019 film "Fly
Me to Saitama" satirized with its exaggerated jokes about the prefecture's
underdog status relative to the capital.

## Getting there and getting around

Omiya Station, a major Shinkansen junction, is about 30 minutes from Tokyo
Station; Urawa is a similar distance via a different JR line. The city's
main sights — the Railway Museum, Bonsai Village, and Hikawa Shrine — are
clustered around Omiya and easily combined into a single day trip.
