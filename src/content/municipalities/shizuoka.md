Shizuoka's most consequential resident chose the city deliberately. Tokugawa
Ieyasu, having unified Japan under his shogunate after 1600, retired here in
1607, ruling as "Ogosho" (retired shogun) from a rebuilt Sunpu Castle while
his son governed formally from Edo — a practical arrangement that let Ieyasu
keep real influence at a comfortable distance from the new capital, in a
region he'd known since his own years as a hostage-turned-vassal of the
Imagawa clan decades earlier. He died at Sunpu in 1616, and the city's
identity as "Ieyasu's retirement home" has anchored its historical branding
ever since.

### Japan's tea capital, almost by accident of geography

Shizuoka's mild climate, hilly terrain, and reliable rainfall turned out to
be close to ideal for tea cultivation, and by the late 19th and 20th
centuries the wider Shizuoka region had grown into Japan's largest green-tea
producing area by volume — a status the 2003 merger that folded a large
swath of mountainous farmland into Shizuoka City limits made especially
visible, since much of the city's now-considerable land area (second only
to neighboring Hamamatsu among Japan's twenty designated cities) is tea
field rather than urban development.

### A city split between mountains and a mid-sized downtown

That merger left Shizuoka with an unusual profile: a fairly modest, compact
downtown core around Shizuoka Station, wrapped in a vastly larger
administrative footprint of mountains, tea terraces, and small rural
communities. Combined with some of Japan's clearest coastal views of Mount
Fuji, the result is a city whose land area suggests a much bigger place than
its population and downtown density actually reflect.

## Getting there and getting around

Shizuoka Station is a Tokaido Shinkansen stop, roughly 1 hour from Tokyo,
making it an easy day trip; the city's tea-growing hills and Nihondaira
viewpoint require a car, bus, or the Nihondaira Ropeway to reach beyond the
compact, walkable downtown core.
