Modern Fukuoka is the product of an 1889 merger between two adjacent but
historically distinct towns: Hakata, a merchant and trading district with
roots stretching back over a thousand years as a hub for trade with the
Asian mainland, and Fukuoka, the more recent samurai castle town built
around Fukuoka Castle from 1601. The merger vote reportedly came down to a
single vote in favor of the "Fukuoka" name, a decision Hakata's side never
entirely accepted — even today, the city's main train station is called
Hakata Station, not Fukuoka Station, a naming split that still occasionally
confuses first-time visitors.

### Japan's front door to the mainland — and its target

Fukuoka's position facing the Korean Strait made it, for most of Japanese
history, the country's most exposed and most connected point of contact
with continental Asia — a double-edged geography made starkly clear in
1274 and 1281, when Kublai Khan's Mongol invasion fleets landed near
Hakata Bay. Both invasions ultimately failed, the second famously turned
back by a typhoon later mythologized as kamikaze, the "divine wind" — but
not before Japanese defenders, aided by stone walls hastily built along the
coast after the first attack, fought pitched battles on Hakata's beaches.
Sections of those defensive walls still survive along the shoreline today.

### From merchant port to startup hub

That same continental-facing position has, in the 21st century, become an
economic asset again: Fukuoka markets itself aggressively as Japan's
"Startup City," offering streamlined visa processing and tax incentives for
entrepreneurs, and regularly appears near the top of international
livability rankings — a reputation built partly on its food culture (Hakata
tonkotsu ramen and the city's open-air yatai stalls chief among them),
partly on its comparatively low cost of living relative to Tokyo or Osaka,
and partly on being close enough to Seoul and Shanghai to feel genuinely
international.

## Getting there and getting around

Hakata Station is a Kyushu/Sanyo Shinkansen terminus, about 5 hours from
Tokyo and under 3 hours from Osaka; Fukuoka Airport is unusually close to
the city center, just a few minutes by subway from downtown. The compact
city center is well covered by a small subway network and easily walkable
between major districts.
